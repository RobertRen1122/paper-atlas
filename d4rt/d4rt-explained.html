D4RT, Explained

Encode a video once, then ask for any point’s 3D position at any time in any camera — a ViT-g encoder, a cross-attention-only decoder, and one query interface for tracks, point clouds, depth, and cameras

Most feed-forward 4D reconstruction models decode everything, everywhere: a dense per-frame head for depth, another for pose, another for tracks. D4RT (Google DeepMind, arXiv 2512.08924) replaces the heads with a question. A large video transformer encodes the clip once into a Global Scene Representation F; a small cross-attention decoder then answers, for any query q = (u, v, tsrc, ttgt, tcam), “where is the pixel (u,v) of frame tsrc at time ttgt, expressed in the camera of frame tcam?” Every query is decoded independently, so a point track, a point cloud, a depth map, and camera intrinsics/extrinsics are all just different batches of queries.

How this page is grounded. DeepMind has not released code or weights (the paper says the model is implemented in Kauldron). Architecture and training facts below come from the paper and its appendix. To make tensor shapes concrete I use OpenD4RT, an unofficial PyTorch reimplementation (commit 403290a) that transcribes the paper’s stated hyperparameters and fills in the unstated ones. Every number the paper does not state is marked ◇ and is that repository’s choice — but note that its choices reproduce the paper’s two parameter counts exactly (encoder 1,012 M vs “1 B”, decoder 144.3 M vs “144 M”; verified by instantiating the model). §8 lists everything that is a reimplementation choice or a paper ambiguity.

1The pipeline: encode once, query anywhere

Two stages with a hard boundary between them. The expensive part — a 40-layer ViT-g over 6,145 tokens — runs once per clip. The cheap part — an 8-layer cross-attention decoder — runs once per query, and queries never see each other. That boundary is the whole efficiency argument (200+ FPS pose, 18–300× more tracks per second than prior trackers, per the paper) and the whole flexibility argument (sparse or dense, any reference frame).

2The encoder: a video ViT that alternates local and global attention

The encoder is a Vision Transformer over 2×16×16 spatio-temporal patches with the VGGT recipe of interleaved attention: one layer attends within each temporal patch (256 tokens), the next attends across the whole clip (all 6,145). Inputs are resized to a fixed square, and the original aspect ratio is fed back as a separate token so the model can undo the distortion. The paper’s appendix shows this backbone must be initialized from VideoMAE — training from scratch roughly doubles the depth error (Tab. 11).

3The query token: three coordinates, three timesteps, one patch of pixels

A query is five scalars. Turning it into something the decoder can use takes three embeddings summed into one vector: Fourier features of the continuous (u,v), learned tables for the three discrete timesteps, and — the ingredient the paper credits with the biggest single gain — an MLP embedding of the 9×9 RGB patch around (u,v) in the source frame. That patch gives the query something to match against the encoded video, and supplies low-level detail the 16×16-patch encoder threw away.

4The decoder: cross-attention only, eight times

The decoder is a transformer with the self-attention removed. Each block lets the query token read from F and then run an MLP; queries never attend to one another. The paper is explicit that this is a choice, not a shortcut — adding self-attention between queries caused “major performance drops” in early experiments, because at inference the set of queries is arbitrary (one, a sparse handful, or every pixel) and a model that had learned to use its neighbours would be out of distribution. Independence is also what makes training cheap: 2,048 sampled queries supervise a 1 B encoder.

5One interface, five tasks

Nothing downstream is a new head. Each task is a pattern of which query fields you sweep and which you pin, plus — for cameras — a few lines of classical geometry on the answers.

6Tracking all pixels without T²HW queries

Because a track from any pixel at any time is one batch of T queries, D4RT can do something dense per-frame decoders cannot: reconstruct the scene including everything that was occluded in frame 0. The naive way is a full track from every pixel of every frame — O(T²HW) queries. The paper’s Algorithm 1 exploits redundancy with an occupancy grid: a track started at an unvisited pixel marks every spatio-temporal pixel it visibly passes through as visited, and new tracks are only started from pixels no track has claimed.

7Worth knowing from the paper (arXiv 2512.08924)

  • Training setup (§4, App. A). ViT-g encoder (1 B) + 8-layer decoder (144 M); 48-frame clips at 256×256; 2,048 random queries per clip; 500 k steps of AdamW (weight decay 0.03), warm-up 2,500 steps to 10−4, cosine to 10−6, gradient clip 10; local batch 1 on each of 64 TPU chips; “just over 2 days”. Implemented in Kauldron. Data: BlendedMVS, Co3Dv2, Dynamic Replica, Kubric, MVS-Synth, PointOdyssey, ScanNet++, ScanNet, TartanAir, VirtualKitti, Waymo Open, plus internal datasets.
  • Query sampling (App. A). Queries come from ground-truth trajectories; 30% are placed near depth discontinuities or motion boundaries (Sobel on depth); the three timesteps are uniform, except ttgt = tcam is forced with probability 0.4 “to improve downstream performance”.
  • Loss (§2.3, App. A). L1 on the 3D point after normalizing both prediction and target by their mean depth and applying sign(x)·log(1+|x|), weighted by the predicted confidence c with a −log c penalty (DUSt3R-style); auxiliaries: L1 on 2D position, BCE on visibility, L1 on displacement, cosine on normals. Weights λ3D=1, λ2D=0.1, λvis=0.1, λdisp=0.1, λconf=0.2, λnormal=0.5. Table 8: dropping the 2D and normal terms hurts depth most; dropping confidence hurts pose most (ATE +0.126).
  • Ablation setting differs from the headline model. Ablations use ViT-L with 6 decoder layers, 300 k steps, batch 16, on Sintel (§4.4). Backbone scaling ViT-B (90 M) → ViT-g (1 B) helps depth and rotation error most (Tab. 9); VideoMAE initialization is essential (Tab. 11); the 9×9 patch is the best size (Fig. 10) and its presence is worth AbsRel(S) 0.366→0.302 and ATE 0.173→0.091 (Tab. 7); ViT-B→ViT-g takes AbsRel(S) 0.319→0.191 (Tab. 9).
  • Speed (Tab. 3, Fig. 3; A100). Full-video tracks sustainable at 24 FPS: D4RT 1,570 vs SpatialTrackerV2 84 vs DELTA 5; at 1 FPS: 40,180 vs 2,290 vs 5,770. Pose at 200+ FPS, 9× VGGT and 100× MegaSaM, with better accuracy. The paper removed unrelated heads from baselines to be fair.
  • Results. State of the art on TAPVid-3D 3D tracking in camera and world coordinates (Tab. 4), Sintel/ScanNet point clouds and video depth (Tab. 5), and pose on ScanNet/Re10K/Sintel (Tab. 6); the only method in its comparison that reconstructs all pixels including dynamic ones (Fig. 4).
  • Long videos (App. B). Overlapping segments stitched by Sim(3) Umeyama on the top-85%-confidence points in the overlap — VGGT-Long’s first stage without loop closure; beats VGGT and π³ on KITTI 1000-frame sequences.
  • Sub-pixel decoding (App. C). With the encoder fixed at 256², querying at the original resolution and cutting the RGB patch from the original-resolution frame lowers boundary error from 3.32 to 2.19 at no extra encoder cost.
  • Lineage. The design descends from the Scene Representation Transformer (SRT) — a set-latent scene encoding queried by light-weight decoders — and the contribution statement credits the SRT-style decoder to Sajjadi and the local RGB patch and tracking-all-pixels to Le Moing.
